Your seed — verbatim (27 Jul 2026)

I think the essay should be around "What can electronic musicians learn from acoustic musicians". My thinking here is as follows. Take a programmer programming a "shaker role". They think of it as "something dense" - in Drumbank (tonebank's sequel), we designate a special "shaker function", which allows you to create dense, repetitive rhythms. Now, if you have an experience of actually holding a shaker, you know that it is light and noisy (and relatively quiet compared to a large drum, although the high frequency cuts well). You understand that playing a big drum takes a lot of energy - to hit it loud you need a big arm movement. So the embodied-cognition principle comes from a knowledge of the physical world - low and loud things take more time to hit than the shaking of a small rattle. If you're a programmer, you might just choose the "shaker function" and assign it a big-drum sound, and call it experimentation. Your space of possibilities is not filtered by what you know about the music. You don't consider pick-up notes being a re-trigger and therefore having a different dynamic than a single, isolated note. An acoustic musician will project their embodied cognition onto the electronic instruments - knowledge about the gesture, the gravity, tuning and being in sync with others, being a part of the ensemble, etc. I think this is a rich territory - I wonder if there is any adjacent literature on the subject (largely-quoted, nothing too obscure or riddled with lingo).

Adjacent literature Matt asked for (accessible, well-cited — Claude, 27 Jul)

Matched to Matt's own examples; marked for accessibility. Verify editions before citing.

Donald Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (affordances & constraints), building on J.J. Gibson's concept of affordances. THE frame for Matt's core claim: a real shaker's physical affordances constrain what you'll do with it; a software "shaker function" strips those constraints, so the programmer's "space of possibilities is not filtered by the music." Norman's physical/logical/cultural constraints map straight onto the argument. Most accessible, most quoted, zero jargon.

Thor Magnusson, "Of Epistemic Tools: Musical Instruments as Cognitive Extensions" (Organised Sound, 2009; free PDF online) + his book Sonic Writing (2019). Instruments as "epistemic tools"; the digital instrument as a "representational artifact" that risks "reinventing musical practice in its image." Nearly Matt's thesis, from the electronic side. Very quotable.

Marc Leman, Embodied Music Cognition and Mediation Technology (MIT Press, 2008). The central academic text: music perception is mediated by the body / action-oriented hearing, and what that means for music technology. The scholarly spine.

Rolf Inge Godøy & Marc Leman (eds.), Musical Gestures: Sound, Movement, and Meaning (Routledge, 2010) — incl. Godøy's chapter "Gestural Affordances of Musical Sound." Gesture as intrinsic to music: the shaker-gesture point, sourced.

David Sudnow, Ways of the Hand (MIT Press; orig. 1978). A phenomenology of the hand learning jazz piano — the embodied knowledge the acoustic player has and projects. Beautiful and readable; the human-side counterweight to the theory.

Deeper / more technical (optional): Andrew McPherson's Instruments Lab (QMUL) on the gesture–sound link in digital instruments (in DMIs that link must be artificially designed, unlike the causal link in an acoustic instrument); Cadoz & Wanderley on "instrumental gesture" and the effort/energy coupling. Already in Matt's world: Ethan Hein (participatory music) and `sources/embodied-rhythm-references.md` (Phillips-Silver & Trainor etc.) are adjacent.

Interview round 1 — the real thesis: access & transmission (verbatim, 27 Jul 2026)

Q was Claude's "constraints = knowledge / limitlessness = loss" provocation. Matt's answer PIVOTS the essay away from that framing toward ACCESS + TRANSMISSION.

I think fundamentally, I am interested in finding ways of facilitating passing embodied knowledge through technology. There are obvious limitations to it - similar to "you can't learn swing from sheet music notation", but I do think that currently, practical musicianship is either reserved to those who have it in their community or in their family - or to those who have access to thorough musical education, which can be pricey and exclusive. Also, institutionalised music education is often very specialist, and originates in European educational philosophy, which brings things like reductionism (studying music separately from movement, from storytelling, etc.) and a certain vision of embodiment, a tradition of music-theoretical thinking which considers certain elements as foundational (academic focus on harmony for example), etc. The beauty of making electronic music is ease of access - one can get creative quite easily. There is no-one to say that you can't say a busy roll on a big drum running through the entire track. Art doesn't have objective rules, and so it's important that we recognise that we are concerned with something more specific. What we are trying to do is get the students out of the abstraction, and make tools that complement, not contradict, in-person education. Obviously, any app is mass-produced, and a one-to-many type of transmission will come with its inherent biases. That said, I don't think we need to throw the baby out with the bathwater - we can't do justice to an in-person music transmission, therefore we should rely on what's already out there. What's already out there is either inacessible embodied knowledge of experienced players (private lessons are costly) or notated music and books which require years of study.

Claude labels — THE THESIS (not "constraints=knowledge"): the essay is about facilitating the passing of embodied knowledge through technology, framed as ACCESS. Chain: (1) embodied musicianship is gatekept — you inherit it (family/community) or pay for it (pricey, exclusive education); (2) institutional music-ed is biased — European educational philosophy → reductionism (music split from movement & storytelling), harmony-centric, a particular vision of embodiment; (3) electronic music's virtue = ease of access / low barrier / "art has no objective rules"; (4) the MISSION = tools that complement, not contradict, in-person education, getting students "out of the abstraction"; (5) honest caveats — you can't fully transmit embodied knowledge through tech ("can't learn swing from notation"), and mass-produced apps carry one-to-many biases; (6) but don't throw the baby out — the alternatives (costly private lessons; years of book study) exclude more people. Ties to Turino low-floor/ high-ceiling + participatory music (participatory-music-turino-notes.md). ⚠ TENSION to resolve for a CLEAR point: two faces of "freedom" — the coder's unfiltered possibility-space read as ignorance (the shaker spill) vs freedom read as democratic access (here). Round-2 Q aims at that.

Interview round 2 — the keystone example: internal timekeeping (verbatim, 27 Jul 2026)

Q was: give one concrete picture of a tool passing a bit of embodied knowledge usefully, even imperfectly, to someone who'll never get the private lesson.

An example would be - from my teachnig experience, it is absolutely impossible to develop a sense of internal timekeeping just by staring at a screen. This is a reference we carry in our bodies. My years of teaching singing and musicianship tell me that this is extremely individual - some of us were allowed to "bounce around" as children, some of us were told to be quiet, etc. - the gist of it is, internal timekeeping only develops with motion - it can't be shown to someone. Someone has to do something. So if you had an app that actually shows you your weight transfer - from one foot into another - especially if combined with some cool music - has the potential to bring your attention back to your own body - to develop a skill you then take with you when you switch off the app. This is a prerequisite for timekeeping, and other things (like clapping off the beat) can then be taught on top of it.

Claude labels — THE KEYSTONE, and it resolves the freedom paradox. Principle: embodied knowledge (internal timekeeping) can't be shown, only done — "someone has to do something"; it develops only with motion. It's individual (some kids "allowed to bounce around," some "told to be quiet" — ties to the shower-closet unlearning-silence + do-birds). The tool = an app that shows your weight transfer, foot to foot, + cool music → brings attention back to your own body → you build a skill you take with you when you switch off the app. Success metric = the tool makes itself unnecessary. It's a prerequisite/foundation (timekeeping) that other things build on (clapping off the beat). CLEAR CENTRAL POINT this crystallizes: a good music tool gets you off the screen and into your body, and succeeds by becoming unnecessary — that's how tech widens access to gatekept embodied knowledge without replacing the teacher or the tradition. Through-line to Matt's whole corpus: weight-transfer/bipedalism = the Tonebank/embodied-rhythm material; "allowed to bounce vs told to be quiet" = the shower-closet's shame/permission.
