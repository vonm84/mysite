Your seed — verbatim (27 Jul 2026)

I think the essay should be around "What can electronic musicians learn from acoustic musicians". My thinking here is as follows. Take a programmer programming a "shaker role". They think of it as "something dense" - in Drumbank (tonebank's sequel), we designate a special "shaker function", which allows you to create dense, repetitive rhythms. Now, if you have an experience of actually holding a shaker, you know that it is light and noisy (and relatively quiet compared to a large drum, although the high frequency cuts well). You understand that playing a big drum takes a lot of energy - to hit it loud you need a big arm movement. So the embodied-cognition principle comes from a knowledge of the physical world - low and loud things take more time to hit than the shaking of a small rattle. If you're a programmer, you might just choose the "shaker function" and assign it a big-drum sound, and call it experimentation. Your space of possibilities is not filtered by what you know about the music. You don't consider pick-up notes being a re-trigger and therefore having a different dynamic than a single, isolated note. An acoustic musician will project their embodied cognition onto the electronic instruments - knowledge about the gesture, the gravity, tuning and being in sync with others, being a part of the ensemble, etc. I think this is a rich territory - I wonder if there is any adjacent literature on the subject (largely-quoted, nothing too obscure or riddled with lingo).

Adjacent literature Matt asked for (accessible, well-cited — Claude, 27 Jul)

Matched to Matt's own examples; marked for accessibility. Verify editions before citing.

Donald Norman, The Design of Everyday Things (affordances & constraints), building on J.J. Gibson's concept of affordances. THE frame for Matt's core claim: a real shaker's physical affordances constrain what you'll do with it; a software "shaker function" strips those constraints, so the programmer's "space of possibilities is not filtered by the music." Norman's physical/logical/cultural constraints map straight onto the argument. Most accessible, most quoted, zero jargon.

Thor Magnusson, "Of Epistemic Tools: Musical Instruments as Cognitive Extensions" (Organised Sound, 2009; free PDF online) + his book Sonic Writing (2019). Instruments as "epistemic tools"; the digital instrument as a "representational artifact" that risks "reinventing musical practice in its image." Nearly Matt's thesis, from the electronic side. Very quotable.

Marc Leman, Embodied Music Cognition and Mediation Technology (MIT Press, 2008). The central academic text: music perception is mediated by the body / action-oriented hearing, and what that means for music technology. The scholarly spine.

Rolf Inge Godøy & Marc Leman (eds.), Musical Gestures: Sound, Movement, and Meaning (Routledge, 2010) — incl. Godøy's chapter "Gestural Affordances of Musical Sound." Gesture as intrinsic to music: the shaker-gesture point, sourced.

David Sudnow, Ways of the Hand (MIT Press; orig. 1978). A phenomenology of the hand learning jazz piano — the embodied knowledge the acoustic player has and projects. Beautiful and readable; the human-side counterweight to the theory.

Deeper / more technical (optional): Andrew McPherson's Instruments Lab (QMUL) on the gesture–sound link in digital instruments (in DMIs that link must be artificially designed, unlike the causal link in an acoustic instrument); Cadoz & Wanderley on "instrumental gesture" and the effort/energy coupling. Already in Matt's world: Ethan Hein (participatory music) and `sources/embodied-rhythm-references.md` (Phillips-Silver & Trainor etc.) are adjacent.
