Your seed — verbatim free-flow (26 Jul 2026)

Exactly as Matt wrote it, typos and all. ***…*** = Matt's own emphasis.

What if the shower is a closet? What if it's a fucking prison? I mean check this out - it's great, it echos, it reverberates. It brings the voice back to you. It empowers you. The number of people I've encoutered who are like, oh I'm a shower-singer, etc., whenever I speak to them about my choir or my career as a singer - there are SO many. People who can sing, who do sing, but only associate it with very private spaces. This privacy of it. Language itself is a melody, it has a song. Even your voice reading these words silently to yourself has tone. It bumps up and down around the fullstops and the question marks. And so, to sing you don't really need any previous knowledge. Going back to the classic example - children who babble, who just feel joy in going "ba", or "ma". As adults we cringe at the idea. It takes a lot of careful work to set up an environment where adults feel okay to be playful with their voices. What they typically need is reassurance, is recognition. Is the certainty that if they make a sound that is "out of ordinary", whatever it means to them, and then they look in the eyes of the receiving person, that what they will see is acceptance, is curiosity, is interest. I am prone to this. Even in my choir leading, ***I often prepare something, then say something, make a joke or whatever, and then notice a difficulty in myself to look in the faces of the people I'm leading, fearing that I made a fool out of myself or whatever.*** Going back to the shower as a closet - I know the insides of a closet very well as a non-binary person. I know what it's like to have thoughts, images, memories, reverberating in my head, and I also know the helplessness I feel when I think to myself - wow, there's so much beauty, but this will never be shared with anyone. It's exactly this - "this will never be shared with anyone", that makes me feel like it's a waste. To get to a place where I recognise my own voice as a gift, together with finding the words that I want to say, finding the shouts that I want to shout, finding the whispers that I want to whisper - this took years of un-conditioning. I am totally convinced, well I know it deeply, that the ***child that is playful, that is the songwriter, that is the improviser, that is the communicator, is always in us***. I try to make it my practice to create spaces for myself to exercise this. Showers are great, but why is it often used as a defence, as if what I do in the shower was something shameful? This all boils down to shame. Shame of being heard, of someone finding out what your real voice is like. And this might mean - tired, broken, stuttery, uncertain, unstable, shivering. And if we've watched a lot of talent shows, if we associate singing with competivity, we are convinced that this means there is something wrong with us. What we hear is voices that never falter, that never skip a bit, that never sound nasal because of an allergy, that never sound hoarse or sore. ***Polished products for consumption.*** But then if we think about the history of human singing, it has always always always been a communal activity. Voices are meant to be heard. They are the connections we make with the world. ***Crucially, a voice is not just a vibration of the vocal folds. It is an expression of our innermost self.*** For example, right now my voice is a digital one. Its sound is me tapping on my keyboard. This also evokes emotions, images, memories. I can do the same when I improvise words chanting into my microphone - or writing it down on paper. I am working with meaning, I am expressing what matters to me. From my singing experience, I know that there are songs that matter to me (I might not even intellectually know why), and there are songs that I sing because I'm paid for it, or because someone told me to, or whatever. I think it's important to recognise that in some sense, it doesn't matter what notes you're singing, what lyrics you're singing - what matters is your own truth of what you are transmitting, and the relationship with that truth is a relationship you have with yourself. It's what you deem worthy of naming about yourself. It's an act of kindness to fellow beings - putting frequencies out there that resonate deeply with others. To me this is where singing is. Also the idea that if I'm at home and have thin walls and want to sing but I'm to ashamed - or I have imagined that if I make a certain kind of noise I will be judged. This is political. Spaces are political. I don't see this mentioned enough in singing pedagogies - why do I want to sing? What is that place that my voice springs from? What makes me want to put words on a page, or sing them into a microphone? Do I feel valuable, do I recognise the gift that I am to others? Do I recognise the longing that others have to hear my voice? Maybe they have never heard it? Maybe I haven't risked being myself in front of them? Then maybe they don't know me, and maybe that's why I feel isolated? ***They can't take your song from you. Nobody can do it. It's a river that we're born into.*** It comes with being alive. It's about acceptance.

Interview round 2 — the teacher's authority (verbatim, 27 Jul 2026)

Q was: if you're still-in-it too, does that change what your authority IS — is permission more powerful because you're visibly still afraid alongside them?

Well, I think it's important for the teacher to know where they've arrived - to be able to name and honour the time and effort they put into learning their craft - but I think too often singing craft is presented as a "linear progression" - unlocking bridges, evening vowels, etc. - however, there is so much more to it - what happens in between the lessons, who we meet, whether we rest, etc. - the voice is not a muscle as such, the voice is an expression of a living being. If I recognise this myself as a teacher, this puts me in a more honest place - there are some things I know how to do, and there are some things that are challenging for me. This might not strictly have to do with teaching singing - I think in any method-oriented pedagogy the "I don't know" is seen as a great taboo - the teacher is not supposed to not know. However, I think that given my experience, I have a good intuition about what we can try and what works. As for authority - I try to hold and name my fear, also to position myself as a real person, with specific experience from specific traditions. I do not want for my students to project omnipotence on me, and to therefore stop paying attention to their own tastes and sensations. In abdicating objective measures of musical taste (and instead making suggestions for the student to explore, bearing in mind the student might not like them - which is okay, there are other things to do), I also take myself out of the role of a "musical judge". It is extremely important to me that my students learn to trust their own ears and their own bodies. My job is to create a safe environment for exploration, not to mitigate risk by providing ready answers.

(b) "'I don't know' is a great taboo" in method-oriented pedagogy — the teacher-omnipotence critique. Interview round 3 — recalibrating the judge / the theory of change (verbatim, 27 Jul 2026)

Q was: is the real work evicting the judge from the rooms people live in — making the world more like the shower? And when it works, what changed — the room, or something they stopped carrying into it?

Yes, well - I think it's about making the judgment more adequate to the space we are in. We all carry generalised baggage - being loud in general, being assertive in general - results in consequences. Perhaps it did, or it does, when we were at school or are at work, but we often carry those among our friends or when we're home alone - keeping ourselves small when the space is actually conducive to us playing around and experimenting. If we are in the woods or home alone, being slightly louder than we normally are, or breaking into a song in a conversation with a trusted friend - these are nice ways into liberating your voice. It's almost Pavlovian in nature - we make a rational judgment about what the consequences for us could be in a given space, and then we allow ourselves to try something low-stakes. A tiny, tiny step outside of our comfort zone. Crucially, this step must not be met with any external judgment - even though in our heads we might end up judging ourselves for it. The work is in creating repeated circumstances where it's okay for us to put our voice out there. A memory of this experience is what gets us used to it being okay to play again. It is a slow process of deconditioning - entirely possible, and entirely social. Once we believe we have something to share - our passion, a song, something to say - and once we locate the source of that - we can see more clearly the value of our musical contribution, that which motivates us. If we have that clarity, we are less and less likely to want to stay silent about what moves us, and more inclined to see what we have as a gift to be shared.

Interview round 4 — the title resolved + the close (verbatim, 27 Jul 2026)

Q was the title's knot: is the shower the enemy or the first ally we mistake for the whole house? When does a safe room become a closet — the walls, or the fact it's the only place you're allowed?

"The first ally" is good wording. Yeah, I think whenever someone says "I am a shower singer" it leaves me with a feeling of - but what about all of those people who could hear your music? What would it be like if you let your sound reverberate with others? If the resonance wasn't just with walls, but also with hearts? The advantage of the shower is that it leaves the voice amplified and it makes it linger a little bit - and the fact that it makes you want to sing means that you can hear the beauty of your own voice, and that hearing it brings you something good. If the shower brings you joy, then definitely, absolutely sing under the shower. Sing more under the shower. Sing until you can take the shower song into your living room, with someone close nearby. Don't stop. If you start feeling unsafe, just leave it and go back to the shower. Maybe the shower is not a closet, maybe it's an (un)dressing room - walls mirror differently than people - they bring the vibration directly to you. When you sing to the people, you make their hearts vibrate with exactly the same music.
