Do birds sing, or do they speak?

In my practice with choirs, sometimes we just do an improv with vocal sounds — sirens, improvised words. When I ask the group what we do next, I sometimes hear “sing” — as if the sounds we made didn’t qualify as singing.

The person who says “let’s sing now” might not themself be aware that they are reinforcing a value judgment. What they might actually mean is: “I would like something with more familiar structure, that is more widely recognised as singing in my cultural circles” — obviously a mouthful. But where does that recognition come from?

We are so immersed in equal-temperament music that we don’t recognise anything outside of it as song — or maybe not as “real song.” Someone might hum a melody with little accuracy, but if someone sings that same melody in equal temperament right after, this will get recognised as “the actual song” immediately — because it points at a memory of something that was originally in equal temperament. If I listen to old recordings of Polish folk music, there is no notion of singing in tune or in time, strictly — same with very old blues, or rap, or any other vocal expression that is outside of what the ear is used to. A hum is its own special moment — but it is often neglected as “trying to be like the recording.” “I must sound like the recording” means “I must replicate what is already out there as accurately as possible” — which also means that I judge anything that doesn’t fit my idea of “safe,” in myself and also in others, by proxy. Ever so often I’ve heard people say things like “X has a really good voice — they should go on a talent show,” as if the point of singing was to be judged and to compete. This to me speaks of losing sight of the participative amount of song, and makes me feel sad.

Perhaps a child playing with their voice and inventing rhymes can be seen and recognised as singing — but if an adult does the same thing, that’s just considered them being insane. Rhythmicised speech, which obviously has its own patterning in terms of higher and lower sounds, is rejected as “not music” or “not song” in order to elevate the Western canon. In Polish there is a term radosna twórczość — “joyful creativity” — often used in a derogatory sense: something spontaneous but outside of the acceptable norm; perhaps “cringe” is the closest anglophone idea. Obviously nobody expects whales and children to sing in equal temperament — but an adult is measured against a societal standard. This touches on a more general question of normativity, of associating beauty with sameness, of fear of originality or individuality. Doing something jarring is a risk, and public shaming is something everyone tries very hard to avoid. To negate the playfulness of one’s own vocal expression — inventing rhymes, playing with the sound of words, or even non-verbal sounds produced by the vocal apparatus — is to impose self-judgment: what I do doesn’t qualify as singing.

What I always think of is this: if a speaker can understand the difference between rising and falling intonation in speech — in languages which differentiate it this way, like English — then they have a sense of pitch already. Pitch carries information about meaning, and it is routinely processed every day. So it is a process of sensitisation, of being able to name certain things — to associate “higher” and “lower” with certain internal sensations of sound production — to a finer degree. This is totally trainable. If someone is capable of following the contour of a melody in parallel, it means that they can hear the ups-and-downs of the melody — just that they might not be psychologically ready to transfer it to a vocal range which is outside of their spoken range.

There is a kind of tuning that people are capable of away from their instruments — just intonation, which pretty much all a cappella styles leverage. The intervals are tuned according to their frequency ratios, and you don’t need a piano to get people singing intervals of octaves or perfect fifths — they will often drift to them because they sit comfortably in their range, and because they sound close to a unison. Inexperienced singers with an unexplored vocal range will often sing in parallel fifths to a melody, from my experience, without realising it.

When I hear that in a rehearsal, what I do depends on the context. If it’s a total beginner, I leave it — it’s them finding their voice. Once I’ve built enough rapport, I might point it out, but often I keep it to myself and make a mental note that they need to explore various parts of their range, to see how their voice can “snap” to octaves rather than fifths. What that tells me is that the next step of the work is vocal exploration, not aural training — through play, through “loud, confident and wrong,” through creating opportunities to try new things.

There is an age-old human tendency to distance ourselves from anything animalistic — to construct systems and rules of behaviour that signify to humans that they are human, be it with a dress code, architectural arrangements, or tonal systems. The concept of “in tune” is a tacit cultural agreement. There are many cultures where the focus won’t be on pitch, but more on the rhythmic context and the curve of the line — things that would get edited out in “produced music.”

So what qualifies as singing is situational — and what we believe about what’s permitted shapes what we are willing to try. If we never try, we don’t exercise the muscle, and it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. To “sing like an animal” is considered a slur — unless one creates a specific context where it is permitted. One such context is “democratic circle-singing.” A group of people who trust each other is divided into three sub-groups, and a person from the group gives a loop for each of the three to sing. When they’re done — crucially — they invite another person to the circle and take their place, based on who hasn’t been to the centre in a long time. The group is instructed to stay in time as a whole; what this does is give an opportunity to “even out” the loop, to tune the intervals, to find a resonant consensus — to define what we mean melodically and rhythmically.

And when one creates that context, all of a sudden there is room for innate creativity, for play, for a release of energy.

A vocalisation is a vocalisation. Whether something counts as singing — or as “adequate” singing — is a discernment made by a listener, or by the person vocalising. Do birds sing, or do they speak? If I repeat any phrase, like “cellar door,” a few times, is that speech, or is it song? Who gets to decide? Fundamentally, “was that singing?” is an intellectual judgment — and when we are in the flow, in the middle of a free vocal improvisation, alone or with others or with instruments, we are not in an intellectual-judgment state. The focus is on the joy, on the expression. If it tells my story, it doesn’t matter whether it’s song or speech or grunt or whisper.
